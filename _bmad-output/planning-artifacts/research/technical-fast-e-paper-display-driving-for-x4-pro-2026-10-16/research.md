# technical research: fast e-paper display driving for X4 Pro

**Decision this research serves:** Whether and how crosshatch-player should build a custom fast, per-pixel-driven e-paper display pipeline (short waveform, per-pixel drive budget, partial-transition grayscale) for the X4 Pro and its other targets, learning from Ink-boy and kuslota/InkBoy.

## Executive summary

**What the evidence says to do.** Build InkBoy's technique, not Ink-boy's: an opt-in, SSD1677-only experimental fast mode that loads a one-frame waveform through the volatile LUT command, uses the two RAM planes as a per-pixel hold, black or white selector, keeps a per-pixel drive budget in PSRAM, and re-uploads only the changed window each scan. Do it as a short measurement spike first, then an epic, and never as a replacement for the stock paths. Ship it in 1-bit dithered form; treat InkBoy's dither-free four shades as the second phase, because the method is public and portable but its dose table must be re-tuned per panel.

Four findings drive that answer:

1. **The X4 Pro's SSD1677 has the structure the technique needs.** The datasheet keys each pixel's waveform on the pair of RAM-plane bits into four independently writable LUT rows, accepts one-frame phases, and runs a loaded LUT when the update sequence skips the OTP load; Waveshare's own 4-gray mode on the same 4.26" panel is that exact mechanism [31][32]. The fork's SDK already uploads a 112-byte LUT, activates it with 0xCC, refreshes asynchronously, and writes windowed RAM [9]. The missing pieces are the per-pixel scheduler and the calibration, which InkBoy publishes under MIT [1].
2. **The floor is unknown and cheap to measure.** No source gives the SSD1677 frame period; the datasheet omits it and the family's 5 to 67 ms per frame range cannot be assumed [31][33]. One afternoon with a one-group LUT and a BUSY timer answers it. The SPI side is arithmetic: two 48 KB planes take 38 ms at the datasheet's 20 MHz write maximum or 19 ms at the 40 MHz the driver already runs [31][9] (derived), so a whole-screen 30 scans per second is out of reach at 800x480 and the realistic target is a game or UI window.
3. **Ink-boy teaches lineage, not technique.** It is a 2026-10 copy of CrossInk, its fast preset is gated to UC8279 variant 0x68, its self-reported 276 ms per refresh is slower than the fork's existing 77 to 85 ms DU path on SSD1677, and the driver code was never published [7][9][30]. The head-to-head section below favors InkBoy's structure on every axis that matters for the SSD1677 batch and keeps two of Ink-boy's ideas as instrumentation.
4. **The Sticky can share the code but not the numbers.** It is the same controller at the same resolution on smaller glass, already behaves differently under the X4's DU sequence, and ESPHome's grayscale for it is full-refresh only [9][39]. The Boox Picco is a closed Linux device with no published specs and nothing here applies to it [42].

**Biggest caveat.** Panel damage from short, DC-unbalanced waveforms is documented as a possibility by a peer-reviewed paper and E Ink's patents, but nobody quantifies it [15][16][17]. InkBoy's author has not validated lifetime, panels vary unit to unit, and the fast LUT ignores temperature [1][6][18]. Every safety pattern InkBoy uses (volatile LUT only, stock voltages, session caps, periodic clean, opt-in) is cheap and should be copied before the first scan runs.

## D1. Ink-boy: lineage, what it does, and where its speed comes from

**Answer:** nothing in Ink-boy validates fast driving on the X4 Pro's SSD1677 batch. Its one fast preset is enabled only on UC8279 variant 0x68, its driver body was never published, and it is a descendant of CrossPoint rather than an ancestor: a copy, made on 2026-10-06, of uxjulia/CrossInk v1.6.1, itself a CrossPoint Reader fork under the MIT license [7] (high confidence). It bakes Peanut-GB and Walnut-CGB emulators into the CrossInk file browser for the X4 Pro only, and its code acknowledges the same three controller batches this fork handles: SSD1677, UC8179, UC8279 [7]. GitHub does not register it as a fork; it has 18 commits by one author through 2026-10-15, 8 stars, no forks and no issues [7].

**Where the speed comes from, as far as it is public.** The "faster screen" commit (tag 2.5.0, 2026-10-13) adds a single refresh preset: a host-loaded DU waveform taken from "the stock X3 UC8279 bank", scaled to 40 percent of its frames, the PLL frame-rate register at 0x0F, the display SPI raised from 10 to 20 MHz, a changed-rectangle-only send and drive with unchanged frames skipped, and overlapped (asynchronous) refreshes [7]. The code comment reports roughly 276 ms per refresh against about 680 ms for the stock Fast waveform, "with the darkness of stock and a little ghosting" cleared by a periodic 1.6 s cleaning refresh, measured on one unit [7] (self-reported, unverified). For scale, the fork's existing opt-in 0x1C DU path on the SSD1677 batch takes about 77 to 85 ms per refresh [9], so Ink-boy's best figure is three to four times slower than what this fork can already do on a different batch. The preset is enabled only when the detected controller is UC8279 variant 0x68; SSD1677 and UC8179 units keep stock [7] (verified by re-reading the source).

**The driver itself is not published.** The commit points the SDK submodule at a commit that does not exist in upstream Free-Ink/freeink-sdk or in uxjulia's SDK fork; the author's and Thebitsworld's public profiles and the first of five pages of the SDK's forks hold no SDK fork either [7][30]. Twenty-six minutes later the pointer was reverted to upstream e91e34e, which has `displayBufferAsyncNoShadow` but none of the five tuning methods the activity calls (`setFastRefreshTuning`, `setFastRefreshDuScale`, `setFastRefreshDiffWindow`, `lastRefreshTimings`, `setDisplaySpiHz`); upstream main at a3a920d has none of the six [7][8][30] (high confidence). The HEAD tree cannot build as published. The comment's "1.8.1 betas" is not a CrossInk version (CrossInk tops out at 1.6.1) and the header self-references another project, so the driver was written in a private parent tree [7][29]. The LUT bytes, the frame-scaling rule, the diff-window code, and the timing log are therefore unrecoverable without asking the author.

**A contradiction worth noting.** The README says the custom LUT "drives the e-ink panel outside of its normal operating parameters" and may shorten lifespan; the code comment says drive levels and voltages stay stock and only the frame count and PLL change [7]. Both are the author's words; without the SDK commit neither can be checked.

**Controller-level versus panel-specific.** Portable to any SSD1677 or UC82xx unit: overlapped refresh with the BUSY wait deferred, a changed-rectangle window plus skip-unchanged, differential two-plane updates, SPI at the controller's rated clock, and forced temperature or frame rate [7][8]. Panel-specific: the DU LUT bank itself, the 40 percent frame scaling, PLL 0x0F, the cleaning interval, and VCOM tweaks, which upstream is still tuning on the X4 [8] (see D4). This fork's SDK already has the portable items for the SSD1677 (asynchronous refresh, two-plane differential update with a shadow plane, windowed RAM writes, the 0x1C DU path, custom LUT upload) and the same UC8179 TSSET lever [9].

## D2. kuslota/InkBoy: what the video's method actually is

**Answer:** the driver the video promises is already public. It is not in `esp32-gameboy`, which holds only the Peanut-GB core, storage, and USB gamepad host [2]; it is the sibling repository `kuslota/InkBoy`, released in one commit on 2026-10-12 with the controller gamepad as a third repo [1][3]. The video is "GameBoy on E-Paper ESP32 at 30FPS" on the channel Bo Made It, which reached Hacker News on 2026-10-11 [4][6]. The transcript's numbers (17 steps to 2, roughly 50 ms, a 6 to 8 drive budget, 33 ms scans) match the code: a 17-frame stock partial LUT replaced by a one-active-frame LUT, an 8-pulse default budget, and 33,334 µs image slots [1][5] (high confidence, primary source re-read).

**Hardware.** Waveshare ESP32-S3-ePaper-1.54 V2: a 200x200 panel on an SSD1681 controller, which the author cannot bypass [1]. This is the same controller family as the X4 Pro's SSD1677, not a bare panel [26][35].

**The method, as implemented** (all from the repository, high confidence) [1]:

- Stock partial update costs about 360 ms of controller time. InkBoy loads a one-active-frame waveform through the volatile LUT command 0x32, so one scan drives for roughly 20 ms in dithered mode and 14 ms in grayscale mode; it keeps the analog supply on between scans, saving about 90 ms per update; and it sends both 5,000-byte planes at 20 MHz.
- Per-pixel driving goes *through* the controller: the two image RAM planes (0x24 and 0x26) act as a per-pixel 2-bit selector into the LUT's voltage rows, with 00 = hold, 10 = drive black, 01 = drive white. Each scan uploads a black mask and a white mask; an unmasked pixel receives no drive at all. That is how stationary areas stay clean.
- Dithered mode keeps a 4-bit counter per pixel (four bit-planes, 25 KB of internal SRAM for 40,000 pixels). A pixel whose target changes reloads its budget (default 8 pulses each way); the counter decrements only after a delivered scan; when the last pixel finishes, a final all-hold scan is sent.
- Scheduling is slot-based: an image slot every 33.334 ms anchored to a fixed clock, missed slots skipped rather than queued, the latest frame taken from a three-buffer handoff; the emulator runs at 59.7 fps on the other core.
- Four grays without dithering come from stopping a transition partway. Each of the 12 transitions has its own dose (1 to 12 pulses); the default table from black/dark/light/white is K: -,2,4,7; D: 4,-,1,6; L: 4,1,-,5; W: 7,3,2,-. A per-pixel model tracks an estimated level 0..192, interpolates doses for interrupted transitions, and optionally corrects six round-trip routes. The state lives in PSRAM with working sets copied to internal RAM and rows split across both cores.
- The LUT is Waveshare's stock partial table patched in RAM (frame counts, frame-rate code, VSL selectable from -15 to -9 V, VSH2 for "weak" modes). The OTP command 0x30 is excluded by a build-time safety check, so nothing it does is permanent.

**The calibration process.** The firmware ships a "Transition test" screen that sweeps all 12 transitions as rectangles across four shade bands, a live tuning menu that changes one value at a time without a full refresh, JSON presets on the SD card, and an FPS counter; the README asks contributors for comparison photos and videos [1]. The transcript adds the photo-and-video rig and the many iterations, which the repository does not describe [5] (single source). The author states the defaults are his own tuning for his own panel and that panels vary [1]. Hacker News questions about per-panel calibration and temperature drift went unanswered; the fast LUT does not read the temperature sensor [1][6].

**Reported trade-offs** [1]: more pulses give deeper contrast and ghost clearing but softer motion; fewer give sharper motion and more ghosting; grayscale still leaves faint trails; a dark stain behind moving white objects needs a "weak" mode; heavy scenes drop below 30 fps; long-term panel life is not validated. The driver enforces a cap of 2,402 partial updates per session and a cleaning full refresh every 2,400 unless "continuous play" is enabled.

**What transfers.** The counter-per-pixel idea comes from ZephRay's PaperBoy, which drives raw panel signals; InkBoy's contribution is the adaptation to a controller that only accepts image RAM plus a LUT [1]. Whether the SSD1677 exposes the same plane-to-LUT-row selector is the gating question for the X4 Pro, taken up in D3.

### D2a. Four shades without dithering: how InkBoy does it

**Answer:** a gray is an interrupted transition. InkBoy models every pixel's position between black and white, pushes it toward the target shade with a counted number of one-frame pulses, then holds it; the counts come from a 12-entry table tuned by eye on one panel [1] (high confidence, code re-read).

**The physical trick.** One "dose" is one activation of the one-frame waveform, about 14 ms in grayscale mode. A pixel going from white to light gray gets two doses toward black and is then held with the 00 selector; black to light gray gets four; the full black-to-white swing gets seven [1]. The per-pixel level is a model from 0 to 192 (64 per shade), advanced after each delivered dose by the remaining distance divided by the remaining doses, so the last dose lands exactly on the target [1]. Each 33 ms slot can carry two scans: the drive scan and a follow-up that either drives again (endpoint targets, HOLD off) or holds (gray targets, HOLD on), which "ends the pulse promptly" [1].

**The dose table** (defaults, from black K, dark D, light L, white W) [1]:

| From, To | Dose | HOLD |
|---|---|---|
| K, D | 2 | ON |
| K, L | 4 | ON |
| K, W | 7 | OFF |
| D, K | 4 | OFF |
| D, L | 1 | ON |
| D, W | 6 | OFF |
| L, K | 4 | OFF |
| L, D | 1 | ON |
| L, W | 5 | OFF |
| W, K | 7 | OFF |
| W, D | 3 | OFF |
| W, L | 2 | ON |

The asymmetry is the finding: darkening and lightening the same two shades need different counts, which is why the transcript calls out 12 distinct transitions [5]. A pixel retargeted mid-transition gets a dose interpolated between the two neighbouring shades by its current level, scaled by a retarget percentage; an optional per-pixel history record (origin shade and steps since) corrects six round-trip routes with a return delta and a reversal percentage, defaulting to one fewer dose on L to K to L returns and 150 percent on reversals [1]. A "weak" white-to-light mode drives eligible pixels with VSH2 at 11 or 8.8 V instead of VSH1, which "can fix a dark stain behind a moving white object" [1].

**Memory and compute at 200x200.** Two bytes per pixel of state (level, remaining doses, target, source) plus masks and previous frames, about 100 KB in internal RAM with PSRAM fallback; an optional 120 KB history array in PSRAM; a 20 KB precomputed pulse table; and the planner only re-walks rows whose input changed, split across both cores with identical results verified by a differential host test [1]. Scaled to the X4 Pro's 384,000 pixels that is roughly 770 KB of pixel state and 1.15 MB of history (derived), which only PSRAM can hold.

**What keeps it stable.** Unchanged pixels are never in either mask, so they receive no drive; counters advance only after an acknowledged scan; a hold-only scan ends each burst; a forced full refresh after 2,400 partials and a lifetime cap of 2,402 per session; cleaning refreshes on mode change, game entry and exit [1]. The author's own verdict: it "still leaves faint trails after some shade changes", pausing and restarting a game re-establishes the reference, and the defaults are "the author's own tuning for the author's panel. Panels vary" [1].

**Calibration as shipped.** A transition-test screen shows four shade bands with three 12-pixel rectangles of the other shades sweeping across each for 5 s; the reader judges rectangle shade against background (dose), the track behind it (history), build-up over passes, endpoints and motion blur, changing one value per run; doses and HOLD first, then history routes, then the lightening voltage together with the doses; then a game with thin fast sprites [1]. Defaults are exported from firmware to a JSON preset and a test fails on drift; the planner has a frozen reference implementation and a 9,264-case exhaustive equivalence test [1]. There is no photo, video or reflectance ground truth in the repository; the transcript's photo-and-video rig is the only mention of it [1][5].

**For the game system.** The method is a per-pixel state machine over a two-plane selector, nothing SSD1681-specific except the LUT byte offsets, the frame-rate nibble and the 5,000-byte planes [1]; D3 shows the SSD1677 has the same selector and a 112-byte LUT with the same four voltage rows [31]. The port cost is the dose table, which must be re-tuned per panel, and the memory, which forces PSRAM.

## D3. Prior art and what the X4 Pro's controllers permit

**Answer:** every fast per-pixel e-paper precedent except InkBoy drives a bare panel; the X4 Pro cannot do that. But the SSD1677 datasheet shows the controller has the exact structure InkBoy exploits on the SSD1681, so the technique ports in principle. What no source gives is the frame period, which sets the floor.

**Bare-panel prior art, and why it does not transfer directly.** ZephRay's PaperBoy on the M5Stack PaperS3 reaches 30 to 60 fps by driving the 960x540 panel's raw row and column interface and skipping the waveform method entirely [14] (secondary reporting, medium confidence). His Glider README describes the fundamental per-pixel method: scan the TFT array at 50 to 120 Hz, push each changed pixel toward white or black, count frames and stop after a set period, and never drive pixels that did not change [12]. epdiy does the same with per-panel frame-time curves, a 4 to 5 frame binary mode, and temperature-dependent grayscale [13]. The same README rates integrated-controller panels at 100 to 300 ms per black and white partial update with total latency "from 100ms to several seconds" because of the host-to-controller data path [12]. The X4 Pro's panel has a controller on every batch, so this fork's only route is through the controller's LUT, as InkBoy did.

**What the SSD1677 exposes** (Solomon Systech datasheet Rev 1.0) [31]:

- A 112-byte waveform set: five LUTs of 10 groups with four 2-bit phases each (VSS, VSH1, VSL, VSH2), ten groups of frame counts TP A to D and a repeat count RP, five frame-rate bytes, then gate voltage, VSH1, VSH2, VSL and VCOM. Command 0x32 writes the first 105 bytes; voltages go through 0x03, 0x04 and 0x2C (high confidence). This is byte-for-byte the layout the fork's SDK already uploads for its grayscale LUT, and at the SDK revision checked out here the voltage tail is the same five bytes Waveshare ships; upstream has since moved the VCOM byte after ghosting on the X4 [9][32][8].
- Per-pixel LUT selection keyed on the pair of RAM bits (RED plane, BW plane): 00 to LUT0, 01 to LUT1, 10 to LUT2, 11 to LUT3, with LUT4 driving VCOM. The datasheet's "LUT2 = LUT0, LUT3 = LUT1" note for black and white panels is a waveform convention, not a hardware limit: Waveshare's 4-gray mode fills all four rows differently and activates them with 0x22 = 0xC7, which skips the OTP LUT load [31][32] (high confidence). That is InkBoy's selector (00 hold, 10 black, 01 white) in the SSD1677's own terms.
- Compared with InkBoy's SSD1681: the same 0x32 command and 2-bit voltage-select phases across the SSD168x family [26], with mechanical differences only, 10 phase groups instead of 12, five-byte timing rows, and 105 LUT bytes instead of 153 [35].
- Frame counts of 0 skip a phase and 1 is one frame; no minimum is stated, so a one-frame LUT is inside the documented ranges [31] (medium confidence on legality, by absence of a stated minimum).
- Command 0x22 is a stage mask. The datasheet lists 0xF7, 0xFF, 0xC7, 0xCF, 0x91, 0x99 and a few others by name (high confidence); decoding the bits from those entries, bit 4 loads the OTP LUT and overwrites a 0x32 LUT, so 0xF7, 0xFF, 0xFC, 0xD7 and the fork's 0x1C DU path all run the OTP waveform, while 0xC7 and 0xCC run the loaded LUT and 0xCC keeps clock and analog alive between scans [31] (the decode of unlisted values such as 0x1C, 0xCC and 0x0C is derived, medium confidence). InkBoy counts the kept-alive analog as worth about 90 ms per update [1]. The fork's driver already uses 0xCC to activate its custom grayscale LUT [9].
- Command 0x37 selects "display mode 2" per waveform set and offers a "RAM ping-pong" for mode 2; the datasheet does not say how mode 2 combines the planes beyond the LUT-index table [31].
- The datasheet rates the SPI write interface at 20 MHz maximum [31]; the fork's SSD1677 driver defaults to 40 MHz [9], twice the rating.
- What the datasheet does not give: any Hz or ms for the frame-rate bytes, no frame-rate table, and the gate-line-width commands 0x3A and 0x3B are reserved. The frame period at 800x480, and so the shortest one-frame update, is unevidenced [31]. The SSD1675 of the same family is adjustable from 15 to 200 Hz, 5 to 67 ms per frame, but that must not be assumed for the SSD1677 [33] (low confidence as a proxy).

**The UC8179 and UC8279 batches.** UltraChip parts keep the waveform in registers 0x20 to 0x24 as VCOM, WW, KW, WK and KK tables of 42 bytes, keyed on the old and new pixel values, with a panel-setting bit choosing register versus OTP LUTs [11][27] and a PLL frame-rate register 0x30 that the fork's UC8279 driver sets to 0x0E on the X4 Pro [8][9]. GxEPD2's partial LUT for the 800x480 GDEY075T7 already sets WW and KK to zero and drives only KW and WK, which is the same "hold unchanged pixels, drive changed ones" selector expressed as old/new pairs [11]. The fork's UC8179 driver uploads 42-byte register LUTs and uses TSSET as the frame-rate lever, and the upstream UC82xx drivers run differential DU with "promote to full every N refreshes" against ghosting [8][9]. So the per-pixel-budget emulation exists on all three batches, in two register dialects, each needing its own panel-tuned tables.

## D4. Implementation reality on the ESP32-S3: floors, risk, and what teams report

**Answer:** the SPI floor is arithmetic only, the panel-side floor on controller parts is unmeasured outside InkBoy, panel damage from DC-unbalanced waveforms is documented but unquantified, and no team has shipped and reported on a custom fast mode over time.

**Throughput floor.** No measured SPI figure for a 48 KB e-paper frame on the ESP32-S3 was found; only arithmetic is available (derived). Wire time for one 800x480 1-bpp plane is 19.2 ms at 20 MHz and 9.6 ms at 40 MHz, doubled for both planes; an older ESP-IDF document limits the original ESP32's SPI to 40 MHz through the GPIO matrix, and nothing found states the figure for the S3 [24] (medium). Scaled from InkBoy, one full two-plane upload on the X4 Pro is 19 to 38 ms of wire time before any waveform runs, which alone caps a whole-screen scan near 25 to 50 scans per second; the per-pixel counter store grows from 25 KB to about 240 KB for dithered mode (four bit-planes plus target over 48 KB), and D2a gives the larger grayscale figures [1][9] (derived).

**Panel-side floor on controller parts.** No SSD16xx or UC81xx project reporting a sustained update rate with a custom shortened LUT was found other than InkBoy itself [1]. The fastest documented hobbyist result besides PaperBoy is 20 fps 4-gray video on a bare 960x540 panel driven over its parallel bus from a stock ESP32-S3, calibrated by "lots of trial and error" against a flatbed scanner, with observers reporting heavy ghosting and grayscale remnants and blacks rendered as grays [19] (high confidence, 2026-07). Vendor and reference figures for the X4 Pro's exact panel class agree on the stock floor: GxEPD2 measured 1,567 ms full and 500 ms partial on the GDEQ0426T82 [10]; Waveshare quotes 4 s full and 0.7 s partial and comments its temperature-forced fast init at 1.5 s [32][34]; the current firmware measures 559 ms fast and 1.5 to 1.7 s full and half with the stock sequences, and about 77 to 85 ms with the opt-in 0x1C DU path [9]; reseller listings quote 0.42 to 1 s partial [25] (reseller figures low confidence, inconsistent among themselves). No published ghosting-versus-frame-count measurement exists for any of these controllers.

**The cheap trick everyone uses first.** On SSD16xx parts the common "fast" mode is not a custom LUT: GxEPD2's GDEQ0426T82 driver, the idfxx SSD1680 driver and Waveshare's 4.26" driver all force the temperature register (0x1A) before the update so the OTP picks a shorter, hotter waveform [10][18][32]. The X4 Pro driver already writes 0x5A there for its half refresh, and the UC8179 batch driver's comment calls its TSSET 0x5A "REQUIRED" for the shorter partial [9]. The idfxx author documents the cost: under-driven blacks that settle to visible gray on the next partial, quality that "varies by panel batch and temperature", and a full refresh every ~10 partials to clear ghosting [18] (high confidence).

**Risk and durability: the evidence.** Two independent publisher classes say DC-unbalanced short waveforms can damage panels. A 2021 peer-reviewed waveform paper keeps DC balance "to prevent the charge from getting trapped in the driving backplane" and says fast waveforms that ignore it "may cause damage to the EPD" [15]; E Ink patents state electrodes "may be irreversibly degraded" under extreme imbalance and that DC balance costs update times "two to three times longer" than imbalanced drive [16][17] (verified, two publishers). No quantified lifetime or refresh-count limit was found from E Ink or Solomon Systech; a Hackaday commenter's "~10M refreshes per pixel" is unverified [19].

**Risk and durability: the practice.** The one long-run user retrospective found (an Inky Frame refreshed every 10 minutes for months) showed residue that grew over tens of thousands of refreshes and largely cleared with double refreshes, so it was not permanent, but that was a vendor OTP waveform, not a custom one [20] (medium). Vendor guidance converges on the same mitigation: a full clean every 5 to 10 partials (Inkplate) [21], conservative minimum intervals from Adafruit and Waveshare resellers [22], and InkBoy's hard caps [1].

**Calibration practice.** No open camera or photodiode rig was found; the documented methods are InkBoy's on-device transition test plus photos [1][5], Tony Weston's flatbed scanner [19], and epdiy's per-panel frame-time curves generated offline [13]. epdiy notes grayscale accuracy is temperature dependent (calibrated near 22 °C) and VCOM must be calibrated per module for partial updates [13]; the upstream SDK's own 2026-10-12 commit changed the SSD1677 custom-LUT VCOM byte after ghosting on the X4 [8], which is the same lesson on this panel.

**Team-level reality.** No open e-reader firmware with a 6 to 12 month retrospective on a shipped custom fast mode was found. The nearest evidence is GxEPD2's author offering only a differential black/white refresh as an "experimental" fast path, with a user reporting "tens of milliseconds" updates on a UC8151D and "a few pixels don't get turned on all the way" [23]; Espressif's own SSD1681 component notes that powering the display on reloads the OTP LUT over a custom one [28]; and the open ESP-IDF driver ecosystem is thin, with one driver offering no LUT upload at all [28].

## D5. The Sticky and the Boox Picco against the X4 Pro

**Answer:** the Seeed reTerminal Sticky is the X4 Pro's closest sibling and can share the fast pipeline's code, but not its waveform numbers. The Boox Picco is a closed device about which nothing usable is public.

**Sticky.** It is a 3.97" 800x480 monochrome 4-gray capacitive-touch e-paper on an ESP32-S3R8 with 8 MB PSRAM and 32 MB flash, with a microphone, temperature and humidity sensor and accelerometer, sold at about 50 dollars and promoted with four open firmware projects [40] (medium confidence, press coverage). Its controller is the SSD1677: ESPHome merged an "SSD1677 four-level grayscale for reTerminal Sticky" model in its 2026.10 release [39], and the fork's own SDK records the Sticky as "SSD1677: its 800x480 panel" on the same board family as the X4 Pro, with the same GT911 touch controller [9]. Seeed's own graphics library drives its 3.97" and 4.26" panels with one SSD1677 driver that already has partial-update, old-plane and 4-gray macros [38]. A reviewer measured it against the X4 as the same size and shape with higher pixel density and slightly clearer text, and notes CrossPoint already runs on it in beta [41] (medium confidence). It is not the reTerminal E1001, which is a 7.5" UC8179 panel without touch [37][38].

Two differences matter for a shared fast mode. First, the glass is smaller at the same resolution, so every SPI and memory figure in D4 carries over unchanged, but any dose or frame-count tuning will differ. Second, the waveform already behaves differently: the SDK documents that the X4's 0x1C DU sequence runs the Sticky panel's full OTP waveform at about 1.7 s, so the Sticky uses Seeed's 0xF7 and 0xFF sequences, its own border values, a per-module voltage tail on its grayscale LUT, and a power-up before grayscale phases [9]. ESPHome's Sticky grayscale is full-refresh only, and a black and white partial update "removes the gray levels from the whole panel until the next full update" [39]. So the Sticky confirms the pipeline design (same controller, same LUT structure, same plane selector) and refutes any hope of copying tuning between the two panels.

**Boox Picco.** Press coverage describes a 3.97" grayscale E Ink reader with a front light and microSD, confirmed by Onyx not to run Android and later reported as Linux-based at about 100 dollars for a November 2026 launch; resolution, SoC, display controller, touch and refresh modes are unannounced, no teardown or FCC filing was found, and there is no evidence of user-flashable firmware [42] (medium confidence, pre-launch press only). Nothing in this research can be applied to it unless it turns out to be an open ESP32-class device, which nothing suggests.

## Ink-boy's scaled LUT versus InkBoy's one-frame waveform

Ink-boy keeps the controller's stock DU waveform bank and shortens it: the X3 UC8279 DU bank at 40 percent of its frames with PLL 0x0F, 20 MHz SPI, a changed-rectangle-only send and drive, and overlapped refreshes, while "drive levels stay the bank's and voltages the panel's" [7]. InkBoy changes the waveform's structure, not just its length: a one-active-frame LUT loaded through the volatile 0x32 command, the two image RAM planes repurposed as a per-pixel hold, black or white selector, and per-pixel drive-budget counters deciding which pixels are driven on each 33 ms scan [1][5]. This section was assembled by a fresh-context analyst from the digests alone.

**What the fork already has.** Custom LUT upload via 0x32: exists (the 112-byte grayscale LUT) [9]. Activation without OTP reload via 0xCC: exists [9]; 0xCC decodes as clock and analog on, mode 2, no LUT load, no power-down [31], and Waveshare proves a 0x32 LUT drives an update when 0x22 bit 4 is clear [32]. Async refresh, windowed writes, and a shadow plane for differential updates: exist [9]. The 0x1C DU path at 77 to 85 ms: exists, opt-in, with reported artifacts [9]. UC8179 register LUTs and TSSET 0x5A: exist [9], matching GxEPD2's GDEY075T7 layout [11]. SSD1677 frame-rate control: partial; the SDK forces 0x1A = 0x5A for HALF [9] and the GxEPD2 and Waveshare "fast" paths are the same OTP-selection trick [10][32], but nothing sets LUT bytes 100 to 104 deliberately [9][31]. Missing: per-pixel budget counters, hold/black/white selector rows, a slot scheduler, LUT frame scaling, a diff-rectangle detector, a per-phase timing log, and an SPI clock setter [7][9].

**Verdict.** The evidence favors InkBoy's structure for the SSD1677 batch. Its mechanism maps onto documented SSD1677 features: two planes selecting LUT0 to LUT3 [31], a volatile LUT honoured with bit 4 clear [31][32], and the baseline's existing upload, activation, async refresh and windows [9]; the code is public [1]. Ink-boy's preset validates nothing on SSD1677, its LUT-scaling and diff-window bodies are unpublished, and its only figure is one self-reported unit [7]. Its PLL and TSSET levers are UC82xx registers [8][9], so they belong to the UC8179 and UC8279 batches, where the SDK already has TSSET 0x5A [9]. Still worth taking from Ink-boy: the changed-rectangle send and drive with unchanged-frame skip, the per-phase microsecond timing log and perf CSV as instrumentation, and the periodic HALF clean [7]. The biggest unknown for Ink-boy is the driver itself [7][30]; for InkBoy on the X4 Pro it is the SSD1677 frame period at 800x480 [31] and the per-pixel store scaled from 40,000 to 384,000 pixels (derived in D2a and D4).

**Contradictions the analyst found between the sources.** Ink-boy's README says the LUT runs the panel "outside of its normal operating parameters" while its code comment says voltages and drive levels stay stock [7]; the README credits Thebitsworld for "the custom LUT" while the comment describes the stock X3 bank at 40 percent of its frames [7]; the README presents fast refresh as a device-wide toggle while the code gates it to one UC8279 variant [7]; Ink-boy's 50 ms display period cannot be reconciled with its 276 ms refresh without the unpublished driver [7]; the SDK's SSD1677 default of 40 MHz sits above the datasheet's 20 MHz write maximum [9][31]; and papyrix's "111-byte" LUT disagrees with the datasheet's 112 [31][36].

## Cross-dimension insights

- **The fork is closer to InkBoy than InkBoy was to its own starting point.** InkBoy began from Waveshare's stock driver, whose LUT path serves only the vendor tables [1][35]; the fork's SSD1677 driver already has custom-LUT upload, 0xCC activation, asynchronous refresh, windowed writes and a shadow plane [9]. The work is the scheduler, the counters, and the tuning, not the controller plumbing.
- **The same selector exists on all three X4 Pro batches, in two dialects** (D3). A per-pixel budget layer can sit above both, emitting hold or drive per pixel into whichever plane encoding the batch uses, while the waveform tables stay per batch and per panel.
- **The temperature trick is the lever the fork already pulls** (D4); the next step is the loaded LUT.
- **Scale, not the controller, is the difference from InkBoy.** Ten times the pixels means hundreds of kilobytes of per-pixel state (D2a, D4) that must live in PSRAM behind an S3-only capability guard and does not exist on the C3.
- **The Sticky is the second test bench, not a second target** (D5). Same controller, same LUT structure, same selector, different panel response, so it validates the design and defeats any shared tuning.
- **The one number that decides the ceiling is the one nobody has.** With the frame period unknown, every fps projection for the X4 Pro is arithmetic on SPI time alone.

## Contrary evidence

The red-team pass did not run (`red_team` is off and validation is `normal`). The strongest counter-arguments surfaced by the research itself: the Glider author's own table rates controller panels at "100ms to several seconds" total latency because of the host-to-controller data path [12]; the idfxx author documents that shortened waveforms under-drive blacks and vary by batch and temperature [18]; and E Ink's patents say DC-balanced drive is two to three times slower than imbalanced drive precisely because imbalance degrades electrodes [16]. None of these says the X4 Pro cannot reach 10 to 20 window scans per second; all of them say the result will be grayer, panel-specific, and of unknown long-term cost.

## Recommendations

Each item names what it feeds and the confidence its evidence carries.

1. **Run a frame-period spike before planning the epic.** Feeds roadmap risk and estimates. Load a one-group LUT with TP[0A] = 1, 10 and 100 at the Waveshare frame-rate bytes and two other values, activate with 0xCC, and time BUSY on an SSD1677 X4 Pro; time a full two-plane upload at 20 and 40 MHz with and without the 0x44/0x45 window, and record error rates at each clock, since the driver runs 40 MHz against a 20 MHz datasheet rating [9][31]. Three numbers, one day. Confidence: datasheet structure verified [31]; floor unverified.
2. **Carry the safety patterns from day one.** Feeds PRD constraints. Volatile LUT only with a build check that forbids the OTP write command, stock voltage tail, a cleaning full refresh on a count and on exit, a per-session cap, and an off-by-default setting with a lifespan warning, because damage is plausible but unquantified [1][15][16][17]. Confidence: risk verified by two publishers; magnitude unknown.
3. **Build the InkBoy selector on SSD1677 first, as an S3-only opt-in capability.** Feeds the architecture spine. A scheduler with fixed slots and skip-not-queue, per-pixel 4-bit counters in PSRAM, black and white masks written to the BW and RED planes for the changed window only, a final all-hold scan, and a cleaning refresh plus session cap. Keep voltages at the panel's values and never touch the OTP command. Confidence: InkBoy's code and docs, primary, re-read twice [1]; SSD1677 mapping verified [31][32].
4. **Ship dithered black and white first; port the four-shade model as the second phase using InkBoy's checklist.** Feeds the brief's feasibility and the game system's display contract. The model is a per-pixel state machine (level, remaining doses, target, source) over the same selector, with a 12-entry dose table and hold mask, interpolated retargets, optional history routes and a weak mode; the author took "a lot" of calibration on one panel and it still leaves trails [1][5]. Budget PSRAM for roughly 770 KB of pixel state and 1.15 MB of optional history at 800x480 (derived, D2a). Confidence: method verified from code; tuning per panel unknown.
5. **Port InkBoy's calibration tooling, not its numbers.** Feeds the architecture spine and test strategy. A transition-test screen, a live one-value-at-a-time tuning menu, JSON presets on the SD card, an FPS counter, exported defaults with a drift test, and a frozen reference planner with a differential host test, so each batch and tester can tune without a rebuild [1]. Numbers from a 200x200 SSD1681 do not transfer [1][13]. Confidence: tooling verified from code.
6. **Do not chase Ink-boy's driver; keep two of its ideas.** Feeds the architecture spine. Its only public artefacts are wrapper signatures and a self-reported 276 ms on one UC8279 unit [7]. Take the changed-rectangle send and drive with unchanged-frame skip and the per-phase microsecond timing log as instrumentation; if the author answers an issue, the DU frame-scaling code could save UC8279 work, otherwise the fork's own UC8179 register-LUT path is the better base [9][11]. Confidence: Ink-boy evidence low (single self-reported unit).
7. **Keep the UC8179 and UC8279 batches as a second phase** using the old/new register-LUT dialect with WW and KK held at zero [11][27]; expect separate tuning per batch. Feeds roadmap. Confidence: register layout verified from GxEPD2 and the SDK.
8. **Use the Sticky as the second bench, with its own tables.** Feeds roadmap and test strategy. Same controller and resolution, different panel response and sequences [9][39]; a shared pipeline with per-board waveform tables is the right shape, and shared dose numbers are not. Confidence: controller identity verified by two sources; panel behaviour from the SDK's own notes.

## Open questions

- **SSD1677 frame period at 800x480** and the encoding of the five frame-rate bytes. Answer: the spike in recommendation 1; or a newer Solomon datasheet revision.
- **Does 0x37 RAM ping-pong give a hardware old/new swap** that avoids re-uploading both planes? Answer: datasheet reading plus a bench test.
- **Which 0x22 value to use per scan.** The datasheet decoding points at 0xCC (clock and analog on, mode 2, no OTP load) or 0x0C once powered; a community SSD1677 note for the X4 uses 0x0C for its custom-LUT fast path and quotes timings without a source [31][36]. Answer: the spike.
- **Panel lifetime under one-frame, DC-unbalanced scans.** No vendor figure exists [15][16][19]. Answer: a long-run soak on a sacrificial unit, photographed daily.
- **Per-batch and per-unit variance across the three X4 Pro controllers** and the Sticky's panel, where the X4's 0x1C sequence already runs the full waveform [9]. Answer: the calibration tooling in recommendation 5 on several units.
- **Temperature.** InkBoy ignores the sensor; the OTP waveforms are temperature-indexed [1][17][18]. Answer: test at two temperatures during the spike.
- **Grayscale ground truth.** InkBoy's repository has no photo, video or reflectance data; the dose table was judged by eye [1]. Answer: a fixed camera over the transition-test screen, which also serves the lifetime soak.
- **Measured SPI and DMA cost on the ESP32-S3** for a 48 KB plane; none published [24]. Answer: part of the spike.
