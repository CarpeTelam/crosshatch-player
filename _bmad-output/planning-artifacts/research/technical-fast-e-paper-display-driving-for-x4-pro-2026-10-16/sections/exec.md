## Executive summary

**What the evidence says to do.** The priority is stable four-shade grayscale and flash-free updates for a pen-and-paper game system; frame rate is a bonus. Build InkBoy's per-pixel state machine as the grayscale engine, not as a speed feature: a one-frame waveform loaded through the volatile LUT command, the two RAM planes as a per-pixel hold, black or white selector, and a per-pixel level and dose model in PSRAM that moves each pixel to its target shade and then stops. At a few scans per second, which is all a board game needs, the SPI and frame-period limits that cap InkBoy at 30 fps stop mattering, and whole-screen updates are affordable. Pair it with YACP's no-flash maintenance, which the fork's UltraChip drivers already carry, for the non-game UI. Do a short calibration spike first, then an epic, and never replace the stock paths.

Five findings drive that answer:

1. **The X4 Pro's SSD1677 has the structure the technique needs.** The datasheet selects each pixel's waveform by its pair of RAM-plane bits from four independently writable LUT rows, accepts one-frame phases, and runs a loaded LUT when the update sequence skips the OTP load; Waveshare's own 4-gray mode on the same 4.26" panel is that exact mechanism [31][32]. The fork's SDK already uploads a 112-byte LUT, activates it with 0xCC, refreshes asynchronously, and writes windowed RAM [9]. The missing pieces are the per-pixel scheduler and the calibration, which InkBoy publishes under MIT [1].
2. **The one unknown number is now a calibration input, not a ceiling.** No source gives the SSD1677 frame period; the datasheet omits it and the family's 5 to 67 ms per frame range cannot be assumed [31][33]. In InkBoy's model one "dose" is one one-frame activation, so the frame period sets how finely a shade can be approached and how long a dose takes; it is measured in the same afternoon as the first dose table, with a one-group LUT and a BUSY timer. The SPI side is arithmetic: two 48 KB planes take 38 ms at the datasheet's 20 MHz write maximum or 19 ms at the 40 MHz the driver already runs [31][9] (derived). That rules out whole-screen 30 fps at 800x480 and rules in whole-screen updates at the few scans per second a board game needs.
3. **Ink-boy teaches lineage, not technique.** It is a 2026-10 copy of CrossInk, its fast preset is gated to UC8279 variant 0x68, its self-reported 276 ms per refresh is slower than the fork's existing 77 to 85 ms DU path on SSD1677, and the driver code was never published [7][9][30]. The head-to-head section below favors InkBoy's structure on every axis that matters for the SSD1677 batch and keeps two of Ink-boy's ideas as instrumentation.
4. **The Sticky can share the code but not the numbers.** It is the same controller at the same resolution on smaller glass, already behaves differently under the X4's DU sequence, and ESPHome's grayscale for it is full-refresh only [9][39]. The Boox Picco is a closed Linux device with no published specs and nothing here applies to it [42].
5. **YACP's no-flash maintenance is already in the fork's SDK for the UltraChip batches.** Its trick is a differential refresh whose hold rows give unchanged pixels a weak pulse, using the X3's stock transition waveform; the fork's UC8179 and UC8279 drivers carry the same transition as a grayscale pre-conditioning step, so the game loop can try it there without new waveform work, while the SSD1677 batch has no equivalent yet [9][43].

**Biggest caveat.** Panel damage from short, DC-unbalanced waveforms is documented as a possibility by a peer-reviewed paper and E Ink's patents, but nobody quantifies it [15][16][17]. InkBoy's author has not validated lifetime, panels vary unit to unit, and the fast LUT ignores temperature [1][6][18]. Every safety pattern InkBoy uses (volatile LUT only, stock voltages, session caps, periodic clean, opt-in) is cheap and should be copied before the first scan runs.
